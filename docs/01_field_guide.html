NLP-06 Field Guide

What this project is, what every piece of jargon means and why it is there, what has actually been built so far, and what happens next. Read top to bottom once; after that, use it as a lookup.

1 · Start here

What are we building?

In one paragraph

A hospital system that watches patients' vital signs and warns nurses early when someone is getting worse — and that answers questions about a patient in plain English. The catch, and the entire point of the project, is that it does all of this without patient data ever leaving the hospital. Alongside the working system, we are writing a research paper that measures one specific thing: how much privacy protection you can add before the warning system stops being reliable enough to trust.

Why privacy is the whole problem

If you wanted to build this the easy way, you would send patient vitals to a cloud AI service and get answers back. That is how most AI products work. In healthcare it is close to unusable: patient records are legally and ethically bound to stay under the hospital's control, and once data leaves the building you have lost control of it.

So the design constraint is inverted. Instead of sending the data to the AI, we bring the AI to the data. Everything runs on hardware the hospital owns. Nothing is sent anywhere.

That creates a second, subtler problem. We would still like the warning system to learn from many patients across many wards, because a model that has only seen one ward's patients is not very good. Learning from everyone, while nobody shares data — that tension is what most of the technical machinery in this project exists to resolve.

2 · The shape of it

The system has two halves that meet at one point

People get lost here, so it is worth being precise: there are two separate AI systems doing two different jobs. They are not the same model, and they are not trained together.

Half one — the assistant (the "product track")

A chatbot. A patient or nurse asks a question in plain English; it answers using only that specific patient's records. Built from a language model running locally plus a retrieval step that fetches the right records first.

Job: make a patient's own records understandable and answerable.

Half two — the detector (the "research track")

A small, ordinary machine-learning model. It reads a stream of numbers — heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen, and so on — and outputs a risk score meaning roughly "this person looks like they are deteriorating."

Job: notice trouble early, and be the thing the research paper measures.

They meet at exactly one place, and that junction is the most original idea in the project: when the detector raises an alert, it automatically asks the assistant to explain the patient. The nurse does not just get a red number — they get a short written summary of what is abnormal and what in the patient's history might explain it. Nobody in the papers we are building on has connected these two things this way.

3 · Vocabulary

Every word you will keep hearing

Each card says what the thing is, then why it is in this project. If a meeting confuses you, the answer is almost certainly on this page.

The AI basics

LLM (Large Language Model)

A model trained on enormous amounts of text that predicts what words come next. That simple ability, at scale, is what lets it answer questions and write summaries. ChatGPT and Claude are LLMs.

Why here: it writes the plain-English explanations for nurses and answers patients' questions.

Local / on-premise

The model runs on a computer you physically control, rather than on a company's servers. Works with the internet unplugged.

Why here: this is the entire premise. Patient data cannot leave the hospital, so the model has to come to the data.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

Before asking the model a question, you first look up relevant documents and paste them into the question. Effectively an open-book exam instead of a memory test.

Why here: the LLM has never met your patient. RAG hands it that patient's actual records, so answers are grounded in fact rather than invented.

Hallucination

When a language model states something false with complete confidence.

Why here: in a hospital this is dangerous, and it is the main reason we use RAG and keep the model's creativity setting very low.

Embedding & vector database

An embedding turns a piece of text into a list of numbers positioned so that similar meanings land near each other. A vector database stores these and finds nearest matches — a filing cabinet organised by meaning rather than alphabetically.

Why here: it is how RAG finds the right few paragraphs of a patient's record out of hundreds.

Quantization

Compressing a model by storing its numbers less precisely. Roughly what MP3 does to audio: much smaller, slightly lossy, usually indistinguishable in practice.

Why here: it is what makes an 8-billion-parameter model fit on a 6 GB laptop GPU at all.

The privacy machinery — the heart of the project

Federated Learning (FL)

Instead of collecting everyone's data in one place to train a model, you send the model out to each data holder, each trains on their own data, and only the learned adjustments come back to be averaged together. Like five students studying separately and pooling their notes, never their textbooks.

Why here: it lets the detector learn from many wards' patients without any ward's raw data ever moving.

FedAvg

The standard recipe for the "average them together" step, weighted by how much data each participant had.

Why here: it is the specific algorithm our federated setup uses. Nothing exotic — the default choice.

Gradient / model update

A model learns by nudging thousands of internal numbers ("weights") up or down to reduce its errors. The gradient is the set of instructions for which way to nudge each one.

Why here: gradients are what wards send instead of data — which turns out not to be as safe as it sounds, hence the next card.

Differential Privacy (DP)

Adding carefully calibrated random noise so that no single individual's contribution can be identified in the output. Like blurring a photograph: enough blur and you cannot recognise a face, but you can still see it is a street scene.

Why here: federated learning alone leaks. Researchers have shown you can partly reconstruct individuals' data from gradients. DP is a mathematical guarantee against that, not just a hope.

Epsilon (ε) — the privacy budget

The dial controlling how much noise gets added. Low ε = lots of noise = strong privacy but a less accurate model. High ε = little noise = weaker privacy but better accuracy.

Why here: ε is the independent variable of the whole paper. We sweep it and measure where accuracy collapses.

DP-SGD

The specific procedure for training with differential privacy: cap how much any single example can influence the update ("clipping"), then add noise.

Why here: it is how we actually apply DP during training, via a library called Opacus.

Privacy accountant (Rényi DP)

Training touches the data thousands of times, and each touch leaks a little. The accountant is the bookkeeping that tracks total privacy spent. Rényi accounting is a tighter method than naive addition, so you get more useful training for the same budget.

Why here: without it we cannot honestly state a final ε. We already found and fixed a bug where this bookkeeping silently reset.

The models and the numbers

LSTM & GRU

Neural networks with memory. They read a sequence in order and carry context forward, so they can notice trends rather than just snapshots.

Why here: deterioration is a trend — a heart rate climbing over hours matters more than any single reading.

CNN

A pattern-spotter. It scans for local shapes rather than tracking order over time.

Why here: it is the comparison baseline. Our second research contribution asks whether memory-based models cope with DP noise better than CNNs do.

Sliding window

Instead of feeding one reading at a time, you feed the last 12 consecutive readings as one snapshot, then slide forward by one and repeat.

Why here: it is the input format for the detector — and it is exactly what our Synthea data failed to provide.

Prediction horizon

How far into the future the model is asked to see. A horizon of six hours means: given the last twelve hours, will this patient be deteriorating six hours from now?

Why here: we measured that asking about the very next hour is nearly trivial — the answer is almost visible in the input already. Six hours ahead is a real forecast, matches the clinical purpose of early warning, and leaves room for the privacy experiment to show something.

Patient-level split

When dividing data into training and testing sets, keeping all of one patient's records on one side. The alternative — splitting individual windows at random — scatters one patient across both.

Why here: the deployed system meets patients it has never seen. Testing on unseen hours of familiar patients would answer an easier question than the one that matters.

Non-IID

Jargon for "the participants' data is not all alike." An ICU and a maternity ward see very different patients.

Why here: realistic and harder. Federated learning struggles when participants differ, so testing it honestly means building that difference in deliberately.

Precision, recall, F1, AUC

Ways of scoring a detector. Recall: of the patients who really were deteriorating, how many did we catch? Precision: of the alarms we raised, how many were real? F1 balances the two. AUC summarises performance across all alert thresholds.

Why here: these are the outcome measures of the paper.

False negative rate (FNR)

How often the system misses a patient who really is deteriorating.

Why here: in a hospital the two error types are not equal. A false alarm is an annoyance; a miss can kill someone. FNR is the number that matters most, and we report it separately.

Seeds

Machine learning involves randomness, so the same experiment run twice gives slightly different results. A seed fixes the randomness; running several seeds shows how much the result wobbles.

Why here: a paper reporting a single run is not trustworthy. We report an average across several seeds.

The data

NEWS2

A scoring chart used across NHS hospitals. Each vital sign contributes points; the total tells staff how worried to be.

Why here: it defines what counts as "deteriorating" in our data. Borrowing a clinically validated protocol is far more defensible than inventing our own thresholds.

Synthea

A free generator of realistic-but-completely-fake patient records, produced by simulating people through real clinical care pathways.

Why here: it was our starting dataset because it needs no approvals. It has now failed our tests — see the findings section.

MIMIC-III / MIMIC-IV

Real, de-identified intensive-care records from actual hospitals. Free, but you must complete an ethics course and be approved, which takes days to weeks.

Why here: it is the leading candidate to replace Synthea, because it contains genuine minute-by-minute monitoring.

PTB-XL

A large free collection of real ECG heart recordings, with no approval required.

Why it was here: to prove our findings were not an artefact of simulated data. Now optional — the main dataset is real, so there is no longer a synthetic-data objection to answer. Dropping it frees ~5.5 days of computing for extra repeat runs, which the statistics actually need.

The tools

Ollama

Software that downloads and runs LLMs on your own machine, with no account or internet needed after setup.

Why here: it is how the local language model actually runs.

ChromaDB

A vector database that stores embeddings in ordinary files, no server required.

Why here: it holds each patient's records for RAG, with a hard filter so one patient's search can never reach another's data.

PyTorch

The standard Python library for building and training neural networks.

Why here: the detector models are written in it.

Flower (flwr)

A framework that handles the plumbing of federated learning — sending models out, collecting updates, averaging.

Why here: so we do not write that plumbing ourselves.

Opacus

A PyTorch library implementing DP-SGD and the privacy accountant.

Why here: it applies the noise and tells us the resulting ε. Most of our verified findings so far concern its exact behaviour.

5 · Reality check

What we tested, why, and what it changed

Why we ran tests before building anything

The plan rested on assumptions nobody had checked: that the data had the right shape, that the privacy libraries would work with our chosen models, that the experiment would fit on a laptop. Any one of those being wrong would force a rebuild — and the later you discover it, the more finished work you throw away.

So the first phase deliberately builds nothing. It just asks the eight riskiest questions and answers them cheaply. Each card below is one of those questions: why we asked it, what we found, and what changes as a result. Everything ran on your laptop; the raw numbers are in NLP_06/p0/results/.

Also set up along the way: a Python environment with GPU-enabled PyTorch, Opacus, Flower and the rest; nine test scripts; Java and Synthea generating data; Ollama running two local language models.

Can our models work with the privacy library at all?

broken, fixed
Why we askedTraining with privacy requires tracking each individual patient's influence separately. Not every type of neural network layer supports that, and if ours did not, the entire research track was built on sand.
What we foundThe standard LSTM and GRU layers are rejected outright. The library refuses them and points to special replacements. The model definitions in your research guide would have crashed the first time privacy was switched on.
What changesUse DPLSTM and DPGRU instead of the standard layers. The model code in the research guide must be rewritten before P3B, not during it.

Is the model definition itself correct?

broken, fixed
Why we askedCheap to check, and silent mistakes here do not raise errors — they just make training quietly fail to work, which is the most expensive kind of bug to chase.
What we foundThe models apply a squashing function that the chosen error measure also applies internally. Doing it twice shrinks the learning signal by 4.2×. Training would have looked mysteriously flat with nothing pointing at the cause.
What changesThe models must output raw scores, not squashed ones. One line per model, but it would have cost days to find later.

Does our privacy number mean what the paper will claim?

broken, fixed
Why we askedOne of the paper's four contributions is a provable privacy guarantee across 100 training rounds. That claim is only true if the privacy bookkeeping accumulates across all of them.
What we foundThe natural way to write the code restarts the bookkeeping every round. Over six rounds it reported a flat 0.75 while correct accounting reached 1.83 — under-reporting privacy loss by 2.43×, and the gap widens with more rounds. Crucially it fails in the flattering direction, so it looks fine.
What changesSet the noise level once for the whole run rather than per round, carry each ward's privacy ledger forward between rounds, and report the worst ward's figure rather than the average. Without this the paper would state a false number.

Does our data actually support the method?

the big one
Why we askedThe whole detector depends on reading trends across consecutive readings taken close together in time. If the data does not have that shape, there is no trend to detect.
What we foundSynthea records vitals at appointments, not bedsides. The median gap between readings is 154 days. Of 52 patients, zero had 12 readings within hours of each other. Separately, oxygen saturation is essentially missing (48 readings across all patients) and temperature nearly so (73) — and the NEWS2 scoring we planned to use needs both, so we cannot even label the data.
What changesSynthea is abandoned as the primary dataset. This is the one open decision in the project — see section 6. Note it fails on two independent counts, so fixing the timing alone would not rescue it.

Does the planned experiment fit on your laptop?

does not fit, cut needed
Why we askedThere is no point planning 150 training runs if they would take a year. Better to size the experiment to the hardware now than to discover it halfway through.
What we foundOne full run takes 4.1 hours for the memory-based models and 43 minutes for the simpler one. The planned 150 runs total roughly 22 days of non-stop computing. Separately: the detector needs only 0.03 GB of graphics memory, so it shares the GPU with the language model comfortably — a worry that turned out to be unfounded.
What changesThe experiment must be trimmed. Importantly, the obvious cuts are the wrong ones: dropping model types saves only 17–24%, while halving the number of training rounds saves 50% and is not a loss of scope at all. So the first thing to check is whether training has already converged halfway through.

Can the real-ECG dataset feed our models?

works
Why we askedPTB-XL is our defence against the obvious criticism that everything was tested on simulated data. But it is raw heart-signal recordings, while our models expect tables of vital signs — nobody had worked out how one becomes the other.
What we foundSlicing each recording into segments and computing features per segment (heart rate, variability, signal shape) produces exactly the input format our models already accept. Validated end to end.
What changesThe mapping is settled, so P2 can build it. It also constrains what we may claim: this shows the trend holds on real signals, not that the detector works on real patients. Overstating that would turn our strongest defence into a weakness.

Which way should we build the federated system?

decided
Why we askedThe framework offers two routes — a quick simulated one and a heavier realistic one — and building the wrong one first means building it twice.
What we foundThe simulated route needs a large extra dependency that is not installed. The realistic route is the one the plan already commits to, because the paper claims genuine separation between wards.
What changesSkip the simulation route entirely and build the realistic one directly. Nothing is lost — the separate tests already proved the underlying logic works.

Which language model fits 6 GB — and is the small one good enough?

new · changed the plan
Why we askedYour laptop has 6 GB of graphics memory. The obvious model needs about 5 GB, so it was unclear whether it would run at all — and if a much smaller model were good enough, that would free memory and run faster.
What we foundSpeed: the small 3B model runs 66 words-per-second entirely on the GPU. The larger 8B model runs at 21, with a quarter of it pushed onto the CPU because it does not quite fit. Both are usable — a summary takes about 6 seconds on the larger one.

Quality, and this reversed our expectation: given a real alert about a patient with breathing problems, the small model missed the low-oxygen reading entirely — the most clinically important of the three warnings. The larger model caught it and quantified all three correctly.

And both invented things. The small one fabricated "a history of exacerbations"; the large one fabricated a 24-hour upward trend. Neither appears anywhere in the patient record.
What changesUse both, split by job: the 8B model for clinical alert summaries where a missed finding is a real failure, and the 3B for patient chat where speed matters more and stakes are lower.

And a second, bigger consequence: our automatic hallucination check reported "none detected" for both models, because it searches for a fixed list of suspicious words and neither invention used one. That is direct proof that word-matching cannot detect fabrication. The paper's summary evaluation must verify each individual claim against the source record, exactly as the research guide specified — we now have a worked example showing why, which is worth putting in the paper.

Does the replacement dataset work where Synthea failed?

resolved · unblocked
Why we askedSynthea failed on timing and on missing measurements. Before committing to a replacement we ran it through exactly the same tests, rather than trusting that a better-sounding dataset would actually be better.
What we foundThe blockers are gone. Oxygen saturation — the reading Synthea was missing almost entirely — is recorded in 86% of hours. Every vital the warning score needs is genuinely measured, and 96% of patients stay long enough to form a window.

But there is a catch. Different vitals get recorded at different times, so all six almost never appear in the same hour (under 1% of patients). Carrying the most recent value forward — which is what clinicians do when reading a chart — lifts that to ~80%, giving roughly 800,000 usable windows.

Two surprises. Deterioration is far rarer than assumed: 1.5% of hours rather than the 10–15% planned for. And the two hospitals turn out to differ mainly in what they measure — one records blood glucose twice as often as the other — rather than in their patients.
What changesThe dataset question is settled, and both tracks are unblocked. The paper must declare that values are carried forward and report how often, per vital.

Can we still label deterioration the way we intended?

resolved · better than expected
Why we askedThe new dataset comes with its own label — sepsis onset. Using it would have quietly turned the project into a sepsis classifier rather than the deterioration monitor it was always meant to be. The alternative is to ignore that label and compute our own from the vitals, using NEWS2 — the warning score the project originally planned to use, which Synthea made impossible.
What we foundNEWS2 works on this data, and the numbers land where the original plan expected. Scoring every hour and flagging those at 5 or above gives 12.6% of hours as deteriorating — almost exactly the 10–15% the original documents assumed. Roughly half of patients trigger it at some point.

And we can now check the labels are meaningful. Comparing them against the dataset's clinically-adjudicated sepsis cases, NEWS2 flagged 75% of septic patients in one hospital and 60% in the other at some point in their stay. It fires more often than sepsis alone, which is correct — it is a general escalation trigger, not a test for one condition.
What changesThe project stays a deterioration monitor. NEWS2-derived labels are primary; the sepsis label becomes an independent cross-check.

That cross-check is worth more than it sounds. The original plan was to validate NEWS2 against MEWS — but both are rule-based, so agreement proves only that two rules agree. Checking against real clinical judgement is a genuinely stronger argument, and it turns "our labels are rules, not doctors" from a limitation into a validated method.

One consequence reverses an earlier decision: at 12.6% the class imbalance is moderate, so the originally planned loss weighting is fine after all and AUC-ROC stays informative. Both it and AUPRC get reported.

6 · Resolved

Which data do we build on?

Settled: PhysioNet/CinC 2019. It was downloaded and put through the same tests Synthea failed, and it passed. The comparison below is kept as the record of why, since your mentor will reasonably ask. Both tracks are now unblocked.

What this means in practice

The project remains a deterioration monitoring system — the original intent, unchanged. What changed is only the source of the vitals: real hourly ICU records from two hospitals instead of a simulator. Labels are computed from those vitals using NEWS2, exactly as originally planned, which Synthea could not support because it was missing oxygen and temperature readings.

B · Generate the missing data Write code to invent plausible vitals between appointments, plus the missing measurements. Possible, but you would be generating most of the signal your paper measures. One honest caveat about the federation story

The two hospital systems turned out to be more alike than hoped. Median age 65.5 versus 64, deterioration rate 6.1% versus 5.6%, stay length 39 versus 37 hours — the patients are broadly similar. Where the sites genuinely differ is in measurement practice: one records blood glucose almost twice as often as the other, and respiratory rate coverage differs by twelve points.

That is real institutional variation, and sites disagreeing about what to measure is a genuine obstacle in deployed federated healthcare — arguably a more interesting problem than differing patient mixes. But it is milder than the ICU-versus-maternity contrast the original design imagined. If you want stronger separation between clients, split each hospital further by intensive-care unit or age band.

This still reverses what your prototype guide decided, so bring it to your mentor as a decision rather than presenting it as already done. The licence is confirmed open (ODbL, no application required) — that was checked directly.
